Panel — three palettes

Same geometry, same type, same hierarchy. Only the colour tokens change, so you are comparing palettes and nothing else. Each keeps the rules that made Panel work: flat planes only, one reserved LIVE colour used nowhere else, six family colours held well below it in chroma, and secondary lettering tinted from the plate hue rather than going neutral grey. The preset screen is the new 2×2 mapped to the corner footswitches in all three.

1 · Slate

cool neutral graphite · signal red

Neutral to the point of having no opinion about hue, so the family colours and the red do all the talking. Red is the most culturally loaded "this is on" an instrument has — a red lamp reads as live to anyone who has stood in front of hardware, and it is the strongest signal available against a cool grey plate. The risk is that red also means error; here the fault state is deliberately kept as a dull brick outline so the two never compete.

2 · Ink

deep navy · ice cyan

The marine and aviation instrument convention: a blue-black plate is darker in perceived value than neutral black under warm stage light, which makes the lettering pop harder without raising its luminance. Cyan is the coldest thing on the panel and sits at the opposite end of the spectrum from every family colour, so LIVE can never be mistaken for a block type. This is the most legible of the three at a steep angle, and the one that will look least like a guitar pedal — which may be the point or may be the objection.

3 · Sodium

true black · warm bone · sodium amber

The night-instrument convention, and the one with an actual physiological argument behind it: warm amber illumination preserves dark adaptation, which is why gauge lighting, chart lamps and observatory desks all use it. On a black stage this is the palette that will be easiest on the eyes across a three-hour set, and true black also costs the least backlight bleed on the panel. The warm bone lettering gives it more soul than either cool option — the trade is that amber and the brass amp colour sit closer together than I would normally allow, so the amp family is pushed to burnt orange to keep them apart.
